02 Analysis Description — Step by Step all values live from the inputs · [n] = equation refs used throughout

This section walks the load path from the applied cabin-pressure stress to the tension stress in the main frame and main sill, in the order the sheet computes it. Steps 1–6 are the pressure (Case III) chain; Steps 7–8 add flight body shear (Case I) and combine. Every value shown updates live from the current inputs and carries the same equation reference [n] used in the Supporting Calculations tables.

STEP 1Applied loading — pressure membrane running loads

The fuselage is a thin-walled pressurised cylinder. The cabin pressure differential P acting at radius R stretches the skin in two directions, producing membrane running loads — force per inch run of skin: hoop (circumferential) w = P·R, and longitudinal w′ = P·R/2 (half the hoop value — the classic 2:1 cylinder ratio). Skin stress is simply the running load divided by the skin thickness; these loads exist everywhere in the uncut shell.

The door capability factors ηh, ηl represent any membrane load carried through the door itself. At the default η = 0 the door is a plug carrying none of it, so the full running loads must divert around the opening.

w  = P·R·(1−ηh) = — lbf/in [1]
w′ = P·R/2·(1−ηl) = — lbf/in [2]
Fig. D1. Cabin pressure P generates hoop (w) and longitudinal (w′) membrane running loads in the skin.
STEP 2The cut-out interrupts the load path

In uninterrupted skin the running loads flow in straight lines. The cut-out removes the load path over its width ℓ (hoop) and height h (longitudinal), so the load that used to pass through the opening must flow around it. By symmetry it splits half to each side:

• the interrupted hoop load w·ℓ splits between the two main frames (the vertical edge members fwd & aft of the door) — each picks up a tension PHT = wℓ/2;
• the interrupted longitudinal load w′·h splits between the two main sills (the horizontal edge members above & below) — each picks up a tension PLT = w′h/2.

These are the fundamental cut-out loads. The remaining steps cover how they physically enter the members, and the secondary effects that add to them.

PHT = w·ℓ/2 = — lbf per main frame
PLT = w′·h/2 = — lbf per main sill
Fig. D2. Hoop load interrupted over the door width ℓ diverts half to each main frame (longitudinal load behaves identically, rotated 90°).
STEP 3Diverted load enters the members through skin shear

A skin running load cannot enter a frame or sill as a point force — it transfers by shear along the riveted skin/member interface. The interrupted hoop load is carried sideways into the main frames by shear in the skin panels immediately above and below the opening (the auxiliary bays, depth a upper / b lower). The interrupted longitudinal load transfers likewise through the panels forward and aft of the opening (widths c / d) into the main sills.

Member tension therefore builds up progressively: zero at the auxiliary member, fully developed at the cut-out corner, essentially constant along the door edge, then shed back into the skin symmetrically on the far side. The peak member load — and the peak fastener shear — occurs at the cut-out corners.

qupper = wℓ/2a = — ·  qlower = wℓ/2b = — lbf/in [12]
qfwd  = w′h/2c = — ·  qaft  = w′h/2d = — lbf/in [13]
Fig. D3. Skin shear in the auxiliary bays feeds the diverted hoop load into the main frame; the frame tension diagram peaks at the cut-out corners.
STEP 4Corner fixity — the frame-bending correction

The main frames and main sills form a rigid-jointed rectangular frame around the opening. Pressure bows each edge member inward between its corners, and because the corner joints are continuous they develop moments. Niu idealises the corners at 50% fixity: the fixed-end moments of the two members meeting at each corner (w′h²/12 vertical, wℓ²/12 horizontal [5],[6]) are balanced by one cycle of moment distribution using the stiffness factors K₁ = h/(h+ℓ), K₂ = ℓ/(h+ℓ) [3],[4], giving a single adjusted corner moment Madj.

The skin reacts Madj as a constant shear flow in each corner panel, and that shear drags additional axial load into the members: ½Madj divided by the adjacent bay length — added to each frame (÷c fwd, ÷d aft) and each sill (÷a upper, ÷b lower).

Madj = — lbf·in [10] ·  qcorner (gov.) = ½Madj/area = — lbf/in [11]
ΔPframe = ½Madj/min(c,d) = — lbf ·  ΔPsill = ½Madj/min(a,b) = — lbf
Fig. D4. 50%-fixed corner moments Madj are reacted as corner-panel shear plus extra member axial load.
STEP 5Total pressure tension in each member

Summing Steps 2–4 gives the member tension due to the cut-out [17]; the sheet evaluates fwd/aft frames and upper/lower sills separately and carries the governing side. Two further contributions complete the design load:

• Door-stop check (frame only) — the plug door bears on stop fittings offset S from the frame plane; prying amplification can generate a frame hoop load [16] exceeding the redistribution value, so the worst case [19] takes the greater of the two.
• Adjacent-bay tributary load [18] — each member also carries its ordinary share of shell pressure from the neighbouring uncut bay, P·R·(bay/2).

STEP 6Built-up section — tension stress in frame & sill

The tension is not resisted by the frame or sill alone. At the cut-out edge the load-bearing section is built up from three parts acting in parallel: the member itself, the bear strap (doubler), and an effective width of skin. The total area [21] is the simple sum of the three, and the nominal tension stress is load ÷ area.

Frame bending: the redistributed load arrives through the skin plane, offset ȳ from the built-up section’s neutral axis, so it also bends the section: M = [17]F·ȳ [22], adding M·ȳ/I at the outer chord [23]. (ȳ and I are computed separately for the built-up section and entered as inputs.) The sill is treated as axial-only.

AF = frame + strap + skin = — in² ·  AS = sill + strap + skin = — in² [21]
σframe = [20]F/AF = — ksi ·  σsill = [20]S/AS = — ksi (pressure only)

STEP 7Body shear (Case I) — redistribution around the cut-out

Under flight loads the fuselage skin carries a body shear flow, entered as the four band values qa…qd (or auto-computed from the vertical shear V). The shear in the two bands crossing the cut-out height (qb upper, qc lower) cannot pass through the opening — exactly as in the pressure case, it must divert into the structure around it. The cut-out height splits between the two bands in proportion to the bays: hb = a·h/(a+b), hc = b·h/(a+b).

Niu’s redistribution (Figs 6.5.3–6.5.8) follows from statics on each surrounding panel: the panels forward and aft of the opening carry the door band’s shear in bypass, rising by Δq = q·ℓ/(c+d) [25]; the panels above and below pick up the share shed vertically through the main sills, q₁ = qa + qb·hb/a upper (qd + qc·hc/b lower) [24]; and the corner panels balance the two effects, q₃ [26]. The governing panel’s gross shear stress q/t is the Case I Key Result; the member loads follow in Step 8.

q₁ (above / below) = — / — lbf/in [24]
q₂ (fwd–aft, upper / lower) = — / — lbf/in [25]
q₃ (corners, upper / lower) = — / — lbf/in [26]
Fig. D7. Body shear diverting around the cut-out: fwd/aft panels carry the bypass (q₂), the panels above/below take the share shed through the sills (q₁), and the corner panels balance the two (q₃).
STEP 8Redistributed shear → member cap tension · combine

The diverted shear also loads the members bounding the opening. Along a main sill the skin shear acting on its panel side is not balanced by skin on the door side; the imbalance is dragged into the member as axial load, accumulating from the middle of the door edge toward the corners — exactly as a spar cap picks up web shear. Per Niu §6.4 the resulting cap (liquidating) load is the driving shear flow times half the cut-out dimension: each sill uses its own band (qb upper / qc lower, governing = max); the frames see the mean of the two bands acting over the cut-out height h.

The load is ± — tension at one corner of a diagonal pair, compression at the opposite — and the tension value is carried forward: added to the Step-5 pressure tension and divided by the Step-6 built-up area. The Key Results cards report these combined stresses, plus the frame outer-chord bending of Step 6.

ΔPfr = ½(qb+qc)·h/2 = — lbf [28] ·  ΔPsi = max(qb,qc)·ℓ/2 = — lbf [29]
σframe = ([20]F+[28])/AF = — ksi ·  outer chord = — ksi ·  σsill = ([20]S+[29])/AS = — ksi
Fig. D8. Unbalanced panel shear along the door edge accumulates as cap axial load in the sill — linear from zero at mid-span to ΔP = q·ℓ/2 at the corners (frame analogous over h). The tension corner combines with pressure.

08 Assumptions & Notes

Method & assumptions

  1. Method follows Niu, Airframe Stress Analysis & Sizing, Sections 6.4 & 6.5 — load redistribution around a skin cut-out: fixed-end-moment frame idealisation for cabin-pressure tension, plus flat-plate liquidating-shear for in-plane (flight) shear. [SCT-025]
  2. Door capability factors ηh / ηl default to 0 = full Niu redistribution (the plug door transfers all hoop / longitudinal load to the surrounding frames & sills). Set a non-zero value only with a substantiated door load path — ηh > 0 reduces the redistributed frame load and is unconservative without justification. [deviates from Niu §6.5]
  3. General bays (Niu §6.5): upper a, lower b, forward c, aft d are independent. Each corner panel (a×c, a×d, b×c, b×d) and each side has its own shear / axial — corner shears, edge redistribution and member loads are evaluated per location and only the governing (worst) value feeds the headline. The original spreadsheet assumed equal corners; the full per-corner / per-side breakdown is tabulated in Appendix A (reference only). Frame & sill member axial loads already reflect the different auxiliary distances (frame → c/d, sill → a/b). The default geometry reproduces Niu's worked example: the Case I body-shear chain gives q₁/q₂/q₃ = 1108/932/255 lbf/in (bands = 450, independent of P); the Case III pressure chain reproduces Niu at P = 17.2 psi (corner moment 169,198 in·lb, upper-fwd corner 210.2 lbf/in). Headline values are the governing (worst) location.
  4. Section properties — neutral-axis offset ȳ and second moment of area I must be derived separately from the built-up frame + bear-strap + skin section. Enter the computed values.
  5. Worst-case frame hoop load takes the greater of the stop-fitting load and the cut-out redistribution load. If the stop-fitting load is the lesser, stop loads are insufficient to react frame tension and the frame pulls inward against the door until stop-fitting loads rise to match frame tension (free-edge equilibrium).
  6. Inner-chord stress is lower than nominal hoop due to bending compression, but this is localised at the frame ends; the nominal hoop stress is retained as the conservative peak value.
  7. Body shear (Case I) — the fuselage skin shear flow is diverted around the cut-out per Niu §6.5. Enter four band shear flows: qa (upper aux), qb (upper main), qc (lower main), qd (lower aux). Equal bands → uniform shear (cut-out on the neutral axis, Fig 6.5.3/6.5.4) — validated against Niu's worked example (450 → q₁/q₂/q₃ = 1108/932/255 lbf/in). Unequal bands → variable shear for off-NA cut-outs (floor / cargo, Fig 6.5.6–6.5.8): the cut-out height splits hb=a·h/(a+b), hc=b·h/(a+b) and the upper/lower panels redistribute their own band shear. Headline checks use the governing (worst) panel. [Niu §6.5 Case I]
  8. Band shears from vertical load (optional) — entering a non-zero fuselage vertical shear V auto-computes the four bands from a thin-walled circular-fuselage shear distribution q=V·cosθ/(k·2πR), taking the effective shear area of the skin as the exact thin-ring value k·A = 0.5·A (A = full skin area), giving qmax=V/πR at the N.A. q is max at the N.A. and →0 at the crown. The geometry is circumferential — the cut-out height h, bays and the lower-main-sill offset y0 are arc distances along the skin from the N.A. — so each band's arc position is mapped to angle θ=arc/R (equivalent vertical height y=R·sinθ) before evaluating q at the band centroid. The four band inputs then become read-only. Bare-skin idealisation — not necessarily conservative: a uniform-shell first estimate that ignores the cabin floor beam and stringers; in a real fuselage these divert bending and can raise the side-skin shear above the bare-ring value, so replace qa..qd with an internal-loads / FEM shear-flow distribution (manual entry, V=0) when available. The shear is assumed to act through the section centroid (an offset adds a torsional shear, not included); centroid sampling is within ~3% of the band average. A band reaching the crown (arc ≥ ¼-circle, θ ≥ 90°) is flagged and its q forced to 0. Set V = 0 to enter the bands manually.
  9. Corner peaking — the panel shears q₁/q₂/q₃ and their gross-area stresses are bay-average; the shear migrates to the stiff main sills, so the actual peak at the cut-out corners is ~+50% (two stringer bays, 3:1) up to ~+67% (three bays, 5:3:1). Apply ~1.5–1.67× to the average for local peak skin stress / corner-detail fatigue.
  10. In-plane shear member axial (Niu §6.4, flat-plate / large R) — shear redistributing around the cut-out develops cap (liquidating) axial in the bounding members, axial = q × ½(cut-out dimension): ΔPframe = qfr·h/2 with qfr = ½(qb+qc); ΔPsill = qb·ℓ/2 (upper) or qc·ℓ/2 (lower), governing = max. It is bay-independent (only ℓ, h, the bands and member areas matter), peaks at the cut-out corners, and reduces to q₀·½(dim) when bands are equal. The load is ± (tension on one diagonal corner pair); the tension value is taken and added to the pressure member axial, so the Member Stresses cards read the combined operating member tension. Shear axial is pure cap tension — it adds no extra bending. [Niu §6.4]
  11. Damage-tolerance use: enter P as the operating cabin-pressure differential and the body-shear bands at the typical (1g) flight level — results are operating stresses for crack-growth / residual-strength work, not ultimate design loads.
